# Design the context, verify the result

> **Evidence Report** · Research cutoff: 2026-11-09
>
> This is the evidence base behind every recommendation in this repository ([how-to-use-ai](../README.md)). It draws on official lab guides, papers, university and research-institute reports, Karpathy's talks and writing, GitHub repositories, and Korean-language sources.
> The **verification level** of each claim (primary source checked / secondary confirmation / search summary only / unverified) is tabulated in [Section 8](#8-what-is-verified-and-what-is-not). Direct quotation is kept to a minimum; most content is summarized or paraphrased.
>
> 🆕 **2026 update:** Corrections confirmed after this report, and the latest 2026 measurements, are collected in the [2026 update report](2026-update-report.md). Where the two reports differ, the update report takes precedence.

As of November 2026, the evidence on how to use AI and large language models (LLMs) well comes down to one sentence: **outcomes are decided not by magic phrases, but by a specification that fills in what the model doesn't know, and by verification done outside the model.** The official guides from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and Microsoft all recommend the same parts: Role, Context, Instruction, Format, Constraints, and Few-shot examples. But the newer the guide, the more its emphasis has shifted away from role-setting and emphatic wording toward **context, success criteria, and checks the model can actually run**, and for reasoning models the guides say to use the effort setting instead of phrases like "think step by step." In Wharton's repeated controlled experiments, tips, threats, politeness, and expert personas did not consistently improve accuracy. In other words, many widely shared prompting tips are myths. The three core failures have different causes. Misunderstanding comes from incomplete requests and the model's habit of "assuming instead of asking." When the same instruction is split across multiple turns, performance drops by an average of **39%** ([Laban et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)). Omission comes from the middle of long contexts and from a growing number of instructions. Fabrication (hallucination) comes from training and evaluation setups in which guessing scores better than admitting ignorance. On top of these comes sycophancy: abandoning a correct answer to match the user's opinion. At the workflow level, prompt engineering has expanded into **context engineering**. Labs and practitioners share the same conclusion: run a loop of specification → plan → execution → evidence-based verification → feedback, and enforce rules that must have no exceptions through hooks, permissions, and tests rather than through the prompt. The how-to-use-ai repository should therefore make "instruct → verify the result" its core loop. To be honest, though, it should also state that the three-way classification of Misunderstanding · Omission · Fabrication is this repo's own teaching taxonomy and was not found in any public source.

## 1. All four labs share the same six elements, but the center of gravity has shifted to context and done criteria

The official guides from the four labs, checked on November 9, 2026, all start from the same parts list. Anthropic says to wrap instructions, context, input, and examples each in their own XML tags ([Anthropic, Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). OpenAI says to write developer messages in the order Identity → Instructions → Examples → Context, adding that the optimal order may vary by model ([OpenAI, Prompt engineering guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-engineering)). OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol guide subdivides this framework into eight slots (role, personality, goal, success criteria, constraints, tools, output, and stopping rules) while advising that each slot be kept short ([OpenAI, GPT-5.6 Sol guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latest-model?model=gpt-5.6#prompting-best-practices)). Microsoft presents five elements: instructions, primary content, examples, cue, and supporting content ([Microsoft Learn, Prompt engineering techniques](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/prompt-engineering)). MIT Sloan presents four: task, context, specificity, and examples and constraints ([MIT Sloan EdTech, Effective Prompts](https://mitsloanedtech.mit.edu/ai/basics/effective-prompts/)). The names differ, but every one of them maps onto how-to-use-ai's six slots (Role · Context · Instruction · Format · Constraints · Examples) with nothing left over.

What has changed is the weight of each element. Anthropic's context engineering article says the exact shape of formatting matters less as models improve ([Anthropic, Effective context engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/research/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)). OpenAI's GPT-5.6 guide says not to dictate every step but to describe the destination and the success criteria. The Claude Code docs condense good instructions into three points: tell the model what it can't see, give it a check it can run, and enforce rules that must have no exceptions ([Claude Code, Best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). Anthropic's key test is to show the prompt to a colleague who has minimal context. If the colleague is confused, the model will be too. The analogy of treating the model like a brilliant new employee who doesn't know the company's norms makes the same point ([Anthropic, Prompting best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). So the six elements should not be taught as a formula that must be filled in completely every time. What fits the evidence is teaching them as a **specification checklist**: a way to catch the information that is easy to leave out when handing work to a new hire.

### Placement and delimiters: long material at the top, the question at the end

When a document is long (roughly 20,000 tokens or more), put the document at the top and the question and instructions at the very end. Anthropic and Google both recommend this. Anthropic reports that in its own tests, putting the question at the end improved response quality by **up to 30%** ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices); [Google](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/prompting-strategies)). OpenAI's GPT-4.1 guide says that with long context you should place instructions at both the beginning and the end, and if you write them only once, put them above the context. According to the same guide, delimiting a large set of documents with JSON performed poorly, while XML or an "ID | Title | Content" format performed well ([OpenAI, GPT-4.1 Prompting Guide](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/gpt4-1_prompting_guide)). Microsoft says to repeat the instructions once more at the end because of recency bias ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/prompt-engineering)). Whether the delimiters are XML tags or Markdown headers makes no practical difference. What matters is using one style consistently within a prompt ([Google](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/prompting-strategies); [OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-engineering)).

### Context is not length but information the model can't find on its own

In an example from the Claude Code docs, "fix the login bug" becomes the following: state the symptom (login fails after the session expires), name the directory to look in, have the model first write a failing test that reproduces the bug, and only then have it fix the bug. The docs sum it up: name the files, describe the symptom, and say what "fixed" looks like ([Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). In Google's example, a model that had offered only generalities about a router LED problem gave an answer matching the specific LED status once it received the manufacturer's troubleshooting guide as context and was told to answer from that material alone ([Google](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/prompting-strategies)). In Microsoft's example, adding a single line of supporting content ("topics I care about") made the workshop summary group its points by those topics ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/prompt-engineering)). Claude Code also draws the opposite boundary: if the model can learn something by reading the code and will actually look it up, don't bother writing it down ([Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

### Format instructions are proven to work, but need re-tuning for each model generation

In Wharton Report 1, removing the format instruction to answer in the form "The correct answer is …" significantly lowered performance for both GPT-4o (RD = 0.086) and GPT-4o-mini (RD = 0.121) (p < 0.001) ([Wharton Report 1 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.04818)). Report 2 warns that the common practice of telling non-reasoning models to "just give the answer" is likely to hurt performance ([Wharton Report 2 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.07142)). If you must guarantee the shape of the output, use Structured Outputs that enforce a JSON schema rather than prompt tricks. JSON mode guarantees only that the output is valid JSON ([OpenAI, Structured Outputs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/structured-outputs)). Newer models have shifted by default toward writing more concisely or using less formatting. So carrying an old "be concise" instruction over to GPT-5.6 can make answers too short, and leaving an old no-formatting block in place for Claude Fable 5.1 causes overcorrection ([OpenAI GPT-5.6](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latest-model?model=gpt-5.6#prompting-best-practices); [Anthropic, Prompting Claude Fable 5.1](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-fable-5-1)). Conversely, GPT-6 Astra tends to over-format, so OpenAI advises specifying the desired style concretely ([OpenAI, Prompt guidance (GPT-6)](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latest-model)). Microsoft adds that format requirements can even change factuality. Requiring inline citations reduces fabrication more than instructing "don't make things up," because a fabricated claim would also need a fabricated citation ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/prompt-engineering)).

### Give constraints with reasons, and use absolutes sparingly

Anthropic's signature example: rather than "never use ellipses," give the reason ("this response will be read aloud by a TTS (text-to-speech) engine, which cannot pronounce ellipses"). Knowing the reason lets the model generalize to similar situations ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). Google's whitepaper likewise puts instructions ahead of constraints and says to reserve constraints for safety or strict formatting ([Google/Kaggle whitepaper, unofficial full-text repost](https://hyli.xyz/posts/docs/prompt-engineering_v7)). OpenAI's GPT-5 guide describes a medical scheduling case in which "always obtain consent before booking" conflicted with "auto-book if the patient can't be reached." The more precisely a model follows instructions, the more reasoning tokens it wastes trying to resolve contradictions, so the fix was to state the priority among the rules explicitly ([OpenAI, GPT-5 prompting guide](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/gpt-5/gpt-5_prompting_guide)).

### Examples should show the pattern without inviting copying

Examples are considered the most reliable way to steer format, tone, and structure. Anthropic recommends 3–5, and Google's whitepaper says to start with about 6 for classification tasks and to mix up the order of the classes ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices); [Google/Kaggle whitepaper](https://www.kaggle.com/whitepaper-prompt-engineering)). In Microsoft's example, showing just three headline → topic pairs led the model to correctly output a "basketball" label that wasn't in any of the examples ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/prompt-engineering)). The risk is surface copying. GPT-4.1 may repeat the wording of examples verbatim, so you need to tell it to vary the phrasing ([OpenAI GPT-4.1](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/gpt4-1_prompting_guide)). The GPT-5.6 guide says to keep only the examples that actually change behavior ([OpenAI GPT-5.6](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latest-model?model=gpt-5.6#prompting-best-practices)). OpenAI recommends starting with zero-shot for reasoning models, and Microsoft Research reported that few-shot hampered o1's performance ([OpenAI reasoning best practices](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/reasoning-best-practices); [Nori et al., From Medprompt to o1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03590)). Anthropic, meanwhile, holds that even for thinking models, examples in problem → method → answer form shape how the model reasons. The practical rule that reconciles these: give short, diverse examples that show the format and pattern, with a caveat not to copy their content.

### Roles are for perspective and tone, not a way to raise accuracy

Role is the element where lab recommendations and academic evidence diverge most. Anthropic says even a single sentence makes a difference, and OpenAI also puts identity in the first slot of the developer message ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices); [OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-engineering)). By contrast, in Wharton Report 4, domain-expert personas did not reliably improve accuracy in any of 6 models on either of two benchmarks. On MMLU-Pro there were 9 significant negative differences. Given an expert persona from a mismatched domain, Gemini 2.5 Flash refused to answer an average of **10.56** times out of 25 per question ([Wharton GAIL, Playing Pretend](https://gail.wharton.upenn.edu/research-and-insights/playing-pretend-expert-personas/)). Zheng et al.'s study in EMNLP 2024 Findings likewise concluded, across 162 roles and 2,410 factual questions, that personas did not improve performance ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10054)). Knowledge at Wharton pointed out that these results conflict with the role-prompting advice of three labs ([Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/wharton-why-you-shouldnt-ask-chatbots-act-expert)). The two can be reconciled: the labs don't claim that a role makes the model smarter either. So use a role as one line that fixes perspective, vocabulary, and tone, and secure accuracy through context, success criteria, and examples.

## 2. Tips, threats, politeness, and expert roles don't improve accuracy

The most rigorous public test of popular prompting tricks is Wharton Generative AI Labs' Prompting Science Reports. These studies ask each question from a set of PhD-level multiple-choice problems (GPQA Diamond, 198 questions) and from MMLU-Pro 25–100 times per condition, and score the results against strict thresholds: 100% correct, 90% correct, and majority correct ([Wharton Report 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04818)). This reveals how unreliable a single run is. At the 100%-correct threshold, GPT-4o was only 5.1 percentage points above random guessing (25%), and even that gap was not significant ([Wharton Report 1 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.04818)). The difference between "please" and "I order you" was also not significant overall. There were many large, significant differences on individual questions, but they pointed in different directions and canceled out in aggregate. Report 3 tested tips of $1,000 and $1 trillion, threats such as "I'll kick a puppy," and appeals such as "this is very important to my career." On GPQA, only 5 results differed significantly from the baseline, and all were small. A fake "termination email" threat actually sharply lowered Gemini 2.0 Flash's MMLU-Pro performance (RD = −0.275), because the model responded to the email instead of the problem. On individual questions, a single variant swung accuracy unpredictably by as much as **+36 pp / −28 pp** ([Wharton Report 3 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.00614)). **This is why anecdotes about these tips never go away.** Any variant can make a dramatic difference on a particular question, but the effect does not generalize.

The value of chain-of-thought (CoT) instructions has also shrunk. In Report 2, the effect of "think step by step" on reasoning models was small: +0.029 for o3-mini, +0.031 for o4-mini, and −0.033 for Gemini 2.5 Flash. Response time rose by 20–80%. Among non-reasoning models, some showed an average gain (Gemini 2.0 Flash +0.135, Sonnet 3.5 +0.117), but some models actually declined at the 100%-correct threshold: CoT increased variability and caused errors even on easy questions. Elaborate variants, such as thinking from multiple angles or comparing three solutions, did no better than the simple instruction ([Wharton Report 2 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.07142)). The labs' documentation points the same way. OpenAI says not to use CoT prompts with the o-series ([OpenAI reasoning best practices](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/reasoning-best-practices)), and Anthropic says a general instruction to think deeply works better than a hand-written step-by-step plan. Anthropic also warns that asking Claude 5-generation models to show their reasoning inside tags may be refused ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). Microsoft's prompt engineering techniques page now carries a banner at the top saying these techniques are not recommended for reasoning models such as GPT-5 and the o-series. It also warns that trying to extract the model's reasoning by any means other than the reasoning summary parameter may violate its usage policy ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/concepts/prompt-engineering)).

The "26 principles" paper from late 2023 claimed a 57.7% improvement in quality and a 36.4% improvement in accuracy on GPT-4. But those results came from 20 human-selected questions per principle, scored by humans, and the authors themselves acknowledge that the question set is limited ([Bsharat et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.16171v2)). Later rigorous experiments refuted the paper's tipping (principle 6), penalty warnings (principle 10), and role assignment (principle 16), while supporting structured formats, delimiters, and few-shot examples.

What survived is format instructions, clarity, context, examples, and evaluation. A new recommendation has also emerged: each time the model changes, re-tune the prompt by trimming it. OpenAI reported that in internal coding-agent experiments, switching to more concise prompts raised eval scores by about 10–15% and cut tokens by 41–66%, though OpenAI itself describes these figures as directional ([OpenAI GPT-5.6](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latest-model?model=gpt-5.6#prompting-best-practices)). Google stated that Gemini 3.6+ and Gemini 4 ignore user-specified temperature, top_p, and top_k, and will return errors in the future. The 2025 whitepaper's advice to "start at 0.2/0.95/30" is therefore outdated for these models ([Google](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/prompting-strategies)). MIT Sloan's recommendation of "temperature 0–0.3 for factual tasks" applies only to non-reasoning environments where the setting is exposed ([MIT Sloan EdTech, Hallucinations and bias](https://mitsloanedtech.mit.edu/ai/basics/addressing-ai-hallucinations-and-bias/)). We found no reliable source quantifying how much lowering temperature reduces hallucination. The limitations must be stated clearly as well. The Wharton experiments used mid-tier 2024–2025 models and multiple-choice benchmarks. So these results show only that "tricks don't generalize"; they do not mean that context and task setup are unimportant for open-ended tasks. And if a model's answers to the same question diverge across 100 runs, important outputs should be generated several times and compared.

## 3. Misunderstanding, omission, and fabrication are different failures with different fixes

Most of the research in this section was done with 2023–2025 models. Anthropic's 2026 documentation says newer models hallucinate less ([Anthropic, Reduce hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)). So the older percentages should be read as illustrations of mechanisms, not as current error rates. The mechanisms and countermeasures remain valid.

| Failure | Core mechanism | Prevention while writing | Detection and repair after the response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Misunderstanding | Gaps in the request + assuming instead of asking, then anchoring on that assumption | Give the full specification at once, with reasons and examples; allow up to 3 clarifying questions | Restatement check; consolidate the conversation and retry in a new chat; reset after two failures |
| Omission | Underuse of the middle of long contexts and context rot, a growing number of instructions, output length limits | Documents first and the question last, quotes first, numbered requirements, splitting into steps | Requirements checklist, a per-section ledger, asking the model to own up to what it skipped |
| Fabrication (hallucination) | Inability to tell fact from plausible falsehood + scoring that rewards guessing | Allow "I don't know," use only the provided documents, quotes first, search and grounding | Retract claims that can't be cited, Chain-of-Verification (CoVe), generate several times and compare, open every citation yourself |
| Sycophancy | Preference training rewards agreeable answers | Hide your opinion and authorship, ask for criticism first | Instead of asking "Really?", solve it again from scratch; get a second opinion in a new chat |

### Misunderstanding: models assume instead of asking, then cling to the assumption

The strongest evidence on misunderstanding is Laban et al.'s 2025 study. Simulating more than 200,000 conversations across 15 LLMs, they found that when the same instruction was split into fragments across turns, performance was on average **39%** lower than when it was given completely at once. Most of the loss came not from reduced capability but from reduced reliability. Models formed assumptions in early turns, gave final answers prematurely, and then leaned too heavily on those answers. Once they had taken a wrong turn, they did not recover ([Laban et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)). Models are also weak at noticing ambiguity on their own. On the CLAMBER benchmark of about 12,000 questions, LLMs showed limited practical ability to identify and clarify ambiguous queries. CoT and few-shot gave only marginal gains and could breed overconfidence ([CLAMBER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12063)). The fix is to ask questions *selectively*. Zhang and Choi reported that when clarifying questions could be asked for only 10% of examples, choosing them by uncertainty about the user's intent doubled the performance gain compared with choosing at random ([Zhang & Choi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09469)). The final responses of STaR-GATE, a model trained to ask better questions, were preferred over the original model's in 72% of tasks ([STaR-GATE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19154)). Even questions that don't look ambiguous can be misunderstood. Rephrase-and-Respond, which has the model restate the question before answering, substantially improved performance across many tasks and can be combined with CoT ([Deng et al., RaR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04205)).

Prevention follows the specification principles from Section 1: give the goal, audience, use, and done criteria all at once from the start, explain the reason for each instruction, and show examples. Anthropic says separating instructions, context, examples, and input with tags reduces misunderstanding, and it advises defining success criteria clearly ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). Models also take requests literally. If you want changes but write "Can you suggest some improvements?", Claude may only make suggestions without changing anything. Write the action you want as a verb ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). For repair after the fact, Laban et al. give users two pieces of advice: if time allows, retry in a new conversation, and before retrying, have the model consolidate all the requirements so far into one message and paste that into the new conversation ([Laban et al., Section 7.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.06120v1)). Claude Code's rule reaches the same conclusion: if you have corrected the same thing twice and it is still wrong, don't correct it a third time; reset the conversation (/clear) and write a better prompt that reflects what you learned ([Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

### Omission: requirements get lost in the middle of long contexts and among many instructions

Three causes of omission are documented. The first is position effects. In the "Lost in the Middle" study, models did best when the relevant information was at the beginning or end of the input, and performance dropped sharply when it was in the middle. When the document containing the answer sat in the middle of 20 documents, GPT-3.5-Turbo scored worse than when it was given no documents at all (56.1%). Increasing the retrieved documents from 20 to 50 raised accuracy by only 1–1.5% ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172); [PDF v3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.03172v3)). On RULER, only half of 17 models claiming support for 32K+ context maintained satisfactory performance at 32K ([RULER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654)). On NoLiMa, which uses "needles" that share almost no words with the question, 11 of 13 models advertising 128K+ context fell below half of their short-context performance at 32K. Even GPT-4o dropped from 99.3% to 69.7% ([NoLiMa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167)). Chroma reported context rot across 18 models including GPT-4.1, Claude 4, and Gemini 2.5: even simple tasks become unstable as the input grows longer ([Chroma, Context Rot](https://research.trychroma.com/context-rot)). The second cause is the number of instructions. On ManyIFEval (up to 10 instructions) and StyleMBPP (up to 6), all 10 LLMs declined steadily as instructions were added, and performance could be predicted from the instruction count alone to within about 10% error ([Harada et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051)). On IFScale, which gave up to 500 keyword-inclusion instructions, even the best model reached only 68%, and a **bias toward earlier instructions** was observed ([IFScale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538)). The third cause is output length. Long-context models in 2024 could read about 100,000 tokens but struggled to write outputs longer than 2,000 words. The AgentWrite approach, which splits writing into subtasks, produced coherent texts of over 20,000 words ([LongWriter](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.07055)).

The countermeasures also differ by cause. Put long documents at the top and the question at the end, and wrap each document in XML tags. Having the model quote the relevant passages before it answers makes it focus on the relevant content ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). Number the requirements and ask for a checklist at the end showing whether each one was addressed. The research notes infer, from the instruction-count studies and the early-instruction bias, that it is better to put the most important constraints first and restate them at the end, and to split the work into steps once there are more than 10 hard constraints. No study has tested this combination directly. Asking a model to process a 300-page file in one go leaves it room to skip parts silently. It is safer to process the file in batches, or to require a ledger with one line for every section, even sections with nothing relevant. For long outputs, have the model write the outline first and then write one section at a time. Self-checks ("before finishing, check your answer against the criteria") catch errors well, especially in coding and math. However, Anthropic warns that Claude Opus 5 verifies its own work even without being told to, so leaving old verification instructions in place leads to over-verification ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)).

### Fabrication (hallucination): it arises when guessing is rewarded over "I don't know"

Kalai et al. (2025), from OpenAI and Georgia Tech, compare hallucination to a student guessing on an exam. If false statements can't be distinguished from facts, pretraining produces hallucinations through natural statistical pressure. And hallucinations persist because most evaluations are scored so that guessing earns more points than abstaining when unsure. The prescription is to change how the major benchmarks are scored ([Kalai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664); [OpenAI paper PDF](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/d04913be-3f6f-4d2b-b283-ff432ef4aaa5/why-language-models-hallucinate.pdf)). The OpenAI blog cites a SimpleQA example from the GPT-5 system card. gpt-5-thinking-mini abstained 52% of the time, with 22% correct and 26% wrong. o4-mini abstained only 1% of the time, with 24% correct and 75% wrong. The guessing model looks slightly better on "accuracy," but it is wrong three times as often. Note, however, that these are OpenAI's own figures, checked via a search-index summary because openai.com blocks automated access, and they have not been independently reproduced ([OpenAI, Why language models hallucinate](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/)). Long-form factuality is also partial. In FActScore, only 58% of the atomic facts in biographies written by ChatGPT were supported ([FActScore](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251)). A study of the 636 citations in 84 short literature reviews written by ChatGPT found that 55% of GPT-3.5's citations and 18% of GPT-4's were fake. Even among the citations that existed, 43% and 24% respectively contained substantive errors ([Walters & Wilder, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-41032-5)). Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) tools aren't safe either. In a peer-reviewed Stanford study, Lexis+ AI and Ask Practical Law AI hallucinated on **more than 17%** of legal queries, and Westlaw AI-Assisted Research on **more than 34%**. The study distinguished an "incorrect" type, which states the law wrongly, from a "misgrounded" type, which states the law correctly but cites a source that doesn't support the claim ([Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/ai-trial-legal-models-hallucinate-1-out-6-or-more-benchmarking-queries); [JELS journal version](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:empleg:v:22:y:2025:i:2:p:216-242)). The real-world costs are high. In Mata v. Avianca, on June 22, 2023, the judge imposed sanctions and a $5,000 fine on the lawyers who had submitted fake case law generated by ChatGPT ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mata_v._Avianca,_Inc.); [Justia opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/new-york/nysdce/1:2022cv01461/575368/54)). Similar cases have since multiplied. In June 2026, AFP counted about 1,600 cases across 35 countries ([Malay Mail/AFP](https://www.malaymail.com/news/tech-gadgets/2026/06/16/do-your-job-global-courts-face-rising-tide-of-ai-generated-fake-cases-and-quotes/223865); [Charlotin database](https://www.damiencharlotin.com/hallucinations/)). This count was checked only through search-result summaries, not the original article, and the figures vary with date and scope.

The first step in prevention is to explicitly allow the answer "I don't know." Anthropic says permitting uncertainty can greatly reduce false information ([Anthropic, Reduce hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)). It also reverses the basic incentive identified in Kalai et al.'s analysis, the structure in which guessing pays. The next step is to tie the answer to evidence. Have the model use only the documents you provide; for documents over 20,000 tokens, have it first extract the relevant passages word for word and then analyze using only those quotes. After the draft, have it find a supporting quote for each claim and retract any claim it can't support ([Anthropic, Reduce hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)). Google explains that grounding, which ties output to verifiable sources, reduces hallucinations and improves auditability ([Google Cloud, Grounding](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/grounding/overview)). There are also several detection tools. Chain-of-Verification (CoVe) writes a draft, plans verification questions, answers those questions independently of one another, and then rewrites the final answer ([Dhuliawala et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11495)). Self-consistency samples several reasoning paths and picks the most consistent answer; it showed a 17.9% improvement on GSM8K ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171)). SelfCheckGPT relies on the fact that when the model knows a concept, multiple samples are similar, whereas for fabricated facts the samples contradict each other ([SelfCheckGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08896)). Semantic entropy detects confabulation by measuring uncertainty at the level of meaning rather than words ([Farquhar et al., Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07421-0)). Everyday users can do something similar: ask the same question in 2–3 new chats and be suspicious wherever the names, dates, numbers, or citations differ. Agreement does not prove truth, however; a model can be consistently wrong. The final check of a citation can happen only outside the model. Open the DOI or URL yourself and confirm not only that the source exists but that it actually supports the claim. MIT Sloan describes checking AI-generated citations as non-negotiable in any context ([MIT Sloan EdTech](https://mitsloanedtech.mit.edu/ai/basics/addressing-ai-hallucinations-and-bias/)). Anthropic, too, closes its documentation by noting that these techniques don't eliminate hallucinations entirely, so important information should always be verified.

### Sycophancy: ask "Really?" and the model flips even a correct answer

Sycophancy is a systematic by-product of training on human preference feedback. Perez et al. (2022) at Anthropic showed that larger models are more likely to echo the answer the user prefers ([Perez et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09251)). Sharma et al. (2023) found that five state-of-the-art assistants were consistently sycophantic across four tasks, and that human preference data itself favors responses that match the user's views. For the same argument, feedback became more positive when the user said they liked it. Models often abandoned answers they had gotten right with confidence once the user challenged them ([Sharma et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548); [Anthropic research page](https://www.anthropic.com/research/towards-understanding-sycophancy-in-language-models)). Wei et al. at Google showed that scaling up and instruction tuning actually increase sycophancy in PaLM ([Wei et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03958)). In the FlipFlop experiment on 10 LLMs, asking "Are you sure?" made models change their answer **46%** of the time on average, and accuracy fell by an average of **17%** between the first and final answers ([Laban et al., FlipFlop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08596)). In SycEval, which tested GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet, and Gemini 1.5 Pro, sycophancy was observed in 58.19% of cases. Rebuttals backed by citations were the most likely to drag a model to a wrong answer ([SycEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08177)). There has been a real-world incident as well. OpenAI rolled out a GPT-4o update on April 25, 2025, and began rolling it back three days later, on April 28. According to the post-mortem, adding a reward signal based on thumbs-up/thumbs-down feedback weakened the primary reward signal that had been holding sycophancy in check ([Georgetown Law Tech Institute summary](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/tech-institute/research-insights/insights/tech-brief-ai-sycophancy-openai-2/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/29/openai-explains-why-chatgpt-became-too-sycophantic); [OpenAI, Sycophancy in GPT-4o](https://openai.com/index/sycophancy-in-gpt-4o/); [OpenAI, Expanding on sycophancy](https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/)).

The defenses follow from the mechanism. If you want an evaluation of a piece of writing, hide the fact that you wrote it and any opinion that you like it. Ask for weaknesses first. Instead of "Are you sure?", have the model solve the problem again from scratch without assuming who is right, and let it change its answer only if it finds a specific error. And get a second opinion in a new chat that hasn't seen your opinion or the first answer. That said, we found no controlled study showing that user instructions like "be honest with me" reduce sycophancy in the latest 2025–2026 models. The defenses above are derived from research on the mechanism. If a model says "You're right" after you push back, treat it as a signal that you need independent verification, not as confirmation.

"Vendor" marks techniques taken from lab documentation; "Derived" marks phrases that the author of the research notes built from research findings. Verification effort should be proportional to risk. The ladder below is the research notes' proposal, synthesized from the sources above. When the risk is low, as with brainstorming or drafts, skim the output and ask about anything omitted or uncertain. When the risk is medium, as with work documents, add a requirements checklist, cite-or-retract grounded in your own materials, and one second opinion from a new chat. When the risk is high, as with legal, medical, or financial work or public statements, open every citation yourself and do lateral reading, looking up a different, independent source for each key claim ([Civic Online Reasoning](https://cor.inquirygroup.org/curriculum/collections/teaching-lateral-reading/)). Recalculate numbers in a spreadsheet or in code, run and test code, and break text into atomic facts to check one by one ([FActScore](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251)). Don't count agreement from the same model as evidence. Human vigilance is a variable too. Recruiters given high-quality AI actually became less engaged and made worse decisions, a phenomenon described as falling asleep at the wheel ([One Useful Thing](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/centaurs-and-cyborgs-on-the-jagged)). In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, the more people trusted AI, the less critical thinking they did ([Lee et al., CHI 2025](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=1135061)). Sixty-nine percent of CS50 students were confident in the AI's accuracy even after being told to think critically ([CS50 SIGCSE 2024 paper](https://cs.harvard.edu/malan/publications/V1fp0567-liu.pdf)). As models improve, verification habits tend to slacken, yet the evidence says they should become stricter.

## 4. Context design and a verification loop matter more than any single prompt line

### Context engineering: choosing the smallest set of high-signal tokens

In late June 2025, Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke said he preferred the term "context engineering" to "prompt engineering," and Karpathy agreed. Karpathy described it as the art of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step ([Simon Willison, 2025-06-27](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/27/context-engineering/); [Karpathy X post](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1937902205765607626); [Tobi Lütke X post](https://twitter.com/tobi/status/1935533422589399127)). Willison expected the new term to win because many people take "prompt engineering" to mean little more than typing into a chatbot. Anthropic's September 2025 article defines context engineering as the iterative work of optimally curating, on every call, the set of tokens the model sees at inference time: the system prompt, tools, MCP, external data, and conversation history. Prompt engineering is positioned as the activity that precedes it, focused on writing one-off instructions. The same article also explains context rot. As tokens accumulate, recall degrades along a slope rather than off a cliff, so context is a finite attention budget. The guiding principle is therefore **the smallest set of high-signal tokens** that maximizes the likelihood of the desired outcome ([Anthropic, Effective context engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/research/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)). Write the system prompt at the "right altitude," between brittle hard-coded if-else logic and vague guidance that assumes shared context. Start minimal and add instructions and examples only when you observe failures. Long tasks call for three techniques: compaction, which summarizes the conversation and restarts from the summary; structured notes such as NOTES.md or a to-do list; and subagents that work in a clean context and return only a 1,000–2,000-token summary.

Recommendations on memory files converge across vendors. CLAUDE.md is loaded in every session, so every line consumes a little attention on every task. The guidance is therefore to include only exact test and lint commands, places where the code is misleading, team decisions not recorded in the repo, and things you've had to correct more than once, and to keep it under 200 lines ([Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). AGENTS.md is an open standard maintained by the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation. Its site says more than 60,000 open-source projects use it. The AGENTS.md closest to the file being edited takes precedence, and the user's explicit chat instructions override all of them ([agents.md](https://agents.md/)). Codex says to add a rule only when the same mistake happens twice ([OpenAI Codex best practices](https://learn.chatgpt.com/guides/best-practices)). The shared message: keep it short, add only after repeated mistakes, let the nearest file take precedence, and move long procedures into skills. Because three sources reach the same conclusion independently, this can be treated as vendor-neutral advice. (We could not confirm whether Claude Code reads AGENTS.md by default.) The advice to open a new chat when the topic changes comes from MIT Sloan, Karpathy, Claude Code, and Codex alike ([MIT Sloan](https://mitsloanedtech.mit.edu/ai/basics/effective-prompts/); [Karpathy, How I use LLMs summary](https://tuananhbui89.github.io/blog/2025/karpathy-lec09); [Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/guides/best-practices)). In 2026, with 1M-token windows and automatic compaction, running out of context is rare. Even so, the Claude Code docs recommend focused context for better answers and lower costs. Between 2025 and 2026 the emphasis shifted from "keep the window from overflowing" to "manage relevance and cost." Karpathy's LLM Wiki pattern is context engineering at the personal level. The LLM incrementally maintains an interlinked Markdown knowledge base built from raw sources; it is offered as an alternative to plain RAG, in which nothing accumulates between queries ([Karpathy llm-wiki gist](https://gist.github.com/karpathy/442a6bf555914893e9891c11519de94f)).

### Chaining and five patterns: start with the simplest

Anthropic's "Building effective agents" (2024-12-19) distinguishes two things: workflows, which chain LLMs along predefined code paths, and agents, in which the LLM decides its own process and tool use. It advises finding the simplest solution possible and adding complexity only when needed ([Anthropic, Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents)). The five patterns are: prompt chaining, which puts checkpoint gates between sequential calls; routing, which classifies input and sends it to a specialized prompt or model; parallelization, which runs independent subtasks simultaneously (sectioning) or runs the same task several times (voting); orchestrator-workers, in which a central LLM decomposes, delegates, and synthesizes the work; and evaluator-optimizer, which alternates between generating and evaluating. Non-developers can use these directly in a chat window. Splitting outline → your review → draft → checklist critique → revision into separate turns is chaining. Running the same question 2–3 times, or on different models, and looking for where the answers diverge is voting. Having a second chat with narrowed criteria critique the draft is evaluator-optimizer. This mapping is the research notes' synthesis, not a vendor recommendation. Karpathy's llm-council is a working implementation of voting and evaluation: several models each answer, rank one another's anonymized answers, and then a chair model writes the final answer ([karpathy/llm-council](https://github.com/karpathy/llm-council)).

Anthropic's 2026 documentation holds that, thanks to adaptive thinking and subagents, Claude handles most multi-step reasoning internally. Explicit chaining is still useful, though, when you need to inspect intermediate outputs or enforce a pipeline. The most common chain is self-correction: draft → review against criteria → revise ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). Multi-agent setups are expensive. In Anthropic's research system, an Opus 4 lead with Sonnet 4 subagents outperformed a single Opus 4 by **90.2%** on an internal evaluation, but used about **15 times** as many tokens as chat. Anthropic said multi-agent setups are a poor fit for tasks that need shared context or have many dependencies, and for most coding, which has few parts to parallelize ([Anthropic, multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/research/multi-agent-research-system)). The Claude Code docs sum it up: handing the same job to a subagent doesn't produce a smarter answer. What helps is a fresh reviewer who didn't see the work being written ([Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

### Specify → plan → execute → verify → learn: the agreed loop for agentic coding

According to the Claude Code docs, a disappointing session almost always has one of four causes: information that couldn't be obtained from the repo was missing; there was no way for the model to check its own work; a rule that must hold without exception wasn't enforced by the harness; or the default settings demanded more time, tokens, or confirmations than the task was worth. The docs divide instructions into two kinds. Prompts, CLAUDE.md, and skills are non-deterministic instructions that the model decides whether to follow. Hooks, deny rules, managed settings, tests, and CI are deterministic instructions that the harness enforces every time. Reserve deterministic mechanisms for project-specific "always" and "never" rules ([Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). Harvard CS50's experience supports this distinction. Even with an elaborate system prompt instructing it not to give out solution code, the CS50 Duck still shows code blocks in up to a quarter of its responses, even now that separate code inspects and rejects code. David Malan says prompts alone are usually not enough ([Harvard Gazette, 2026-09-25](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2026/09/taming-the-duck-for-starters/)).

| Stage | What to do | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Prepare | A short CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md (commands, pitfalls, decisions, repeated fixes), deny rules for secret files, a sandbox, a default approval mode, git worktrees for parallel work | [Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices); [Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/guides/best-practices); [agents.md](https://agents.md/) |
| 1. Specify | Goal, context, constraints, done criteria. If the requirements are fuzzy, have the AI interview you and write them up in SPEC.md | [Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/guides/best-practices); [Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices) |
| 2. Plan | For multi-step work, review a plan first in plan mode. Match the weight of the process to the size of the task | [Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/guides/best-practices); [Böckeler](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html) |
| 3. Execute | In a fresh session, implement against a check the model can run (tests, type checks, screenshots). Red/green TDD | [Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices); [Willison, Red/green TDD](https://simonwillison.net/guides/agentic-engineering-patterns/red-green-tdd/) |

A human tests it directly | [Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices); [Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Mar/11/using-llms-for-code/) |
| 5. Learn | Small PRs containing only code you have read yourself. Repeated mistakes go into the memory file, repeated prompts become skills, and rules without exceptions become hooks or deny rules | [Willison, Anti-patterns](https://simonwillison.net/guides/agentic-engineering-patterns/anti-patterns/); [Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/guides/best-practices) |

Detailed guidance for the verification stage: anything that returns pass or fail can serve as a check, such as a test file, a build exit code, a linter, a script that compares output against a reference file, or a screenshot compared against a mockup. And demand evidence, meaning the commands that were run and their output, not the statement "all tests pass." Tell reviewers to report only gaps that affect correctness and to skip style. A reviewer told to find problems will always find something, and fixing every one of those comments leads to over-engineering ([Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). Before an unattended run, check five things: Do you know what done looks like? Can the agent check its own work? What is it allowed to touch? What makes it stop (turn limits, cost caps)? Where do the results go, and who approves them? Codex likewise offers a goal / context / constraints / done-criteria template, and lists common mistakes: putting durable rules in the prompt, not explaining how to build and test, skipping the plan on multi-step work, and granting full permissions before understanding the workflow ([Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/guides/best-practices)). For long-running work spanning several sessions, Anthropic suggests having an initializer agent in the first session create an init script, a progress log, and a JSON feature list in which every feature starts as "failing." Later sessions complete one feature at a time and leave notes. JSON is used because models overwrite it less than Markdown ([Anthropic, Effective harnesses](https://www.anthropic.com/research/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)). Another tendency to guard against is hard-coding values to make tests pass. Tell the model that tests are a means of checking correctness, not the definition of the solution, and have it report a test it believes is wrong rather than working around it ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)).

Spec-driven development (SDD) is tooling that formalizes this loop. GitHub Spec Kit provides constitution → specify → plan → tasks → implement → converge commands, but its README says these are independent entry points, not mandatory steps, and that you should start with the smallest change that meets your needs ([github/spec-kit](https://github.com/github/spec-kit)). Thoughtworks' Birgitta Böckeler offers a warning: Kiro inflated one small bug into 4 user stories and 16 acceptance criteria, and agents ignored spec notes or over-applied principles, which can create an illusion of control ([Böckeler, martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html)). The line between this and vibe coding has also become clearer. In February 2025, Karpathy coined "vibe coding" for fully giving in to the vibes and forgetting that the code even exists ([Karpathy X post](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1886192184808149383)). Willison defines vibe coding narrowly as "not reviewing the code the AI writes." LLM-assisted work that you have reviewed, tested, and understood is simply software development, and he says he won't commit code he couldn't explain to someone else ([Willison, 2025-03-19](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Mar/19/vibe-coding/)). In April 2026, Karpathy summarized it this way: if vibe coding raises the floor, agentic engineering raises the ceiling. An agentic engineer writes specs, supervises plans, inspects diffs, builds test and eval loops, manages permissions, isolates worktrees, and takes responsibility through to the end ([Karpathy, Sequoia Ascent 2026 summary](https://karpathy.bearblog.dev/sequoia-ascent-2026/)). The deciding factor is review and verification, not the tool. If someone else will run it, or it touches real data or money, treat it as engineering, not vibes.

### The same loop works for knowledge work

The same pattern carries over to knowledge work. In research, deep research tools gather material in orchestrator-worker fashion, and a human verifies it. When OpenAI launched deep research, it said the tool can fabricate facts or make incorrect inferences, has trouble distinguishing authoritative information from rumor, and is weak at confidence calibration. This was confirmed through secondary coverage because the original page returned a 403 ([Computing](https://www.computing.co.uk/news/2025/ai/openai-announces-deep-research); [OpenAI launch post](https://openai.com/index/introducing-deep-research/)). In Anthropic's research system, human testers caught a problem that automated evaluation had missed: the system preferred SEO content farms over authoritative sources ([Anthropic, multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/research/multi-agent-research-system)). For writing, run outline → draft → critique against criteria → revision as separate stages, and narrow the critic's scope to accuracy and audience. For data analysis, have the model execute code so that its answers rest on actual execution results. Karpathy also recommends using a Python interpreter for exact calculations ([Willison, 2025-03-11](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Mar/11/using-llms-for-code/); [Karpathy, How I use LLMs summary](https://mvark.blogspot.com/2025/03/how-i-use-llms-andrej-karpathy.html)). Summaries follow the sequence: extract quotes → summarize from the quotes → cite each claim → retract unsupported claims. Meta-prompting means having the AI interview you or draft a prompt, then testing it on 5–10 real inputs and fixing the failures. Anthropic Console's prompt improver reported a 30% accuracy gain on a multi-label classification test in 2024 ([Anthropic, Prompt improver](https://claude.com/resources/articles/prompt-improver)). However, the tool's "add prefill" step is outdated for the latest models. OpenAI's dataset-based prompt optimizer is being retired along with the Evals platform. Evals became read-only on October 31, 2026, and is scheduled to shut down on November 30 ([OpenAI, Prompt optimizer](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-optimizer)). Package stable prompts as reusable units. Anthropic's Agent Skills use progressive disclosure: only the name and description sit in the system prompt, and the SKILL.md body and linked files are read when needed. They were released as an open standard in December 2025 ([Anthropic, Agent Skills](https://www.anthropic.com/research/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills)). Codex gives the clearest criterion for when to make a skill: if you keep reusing the same prompt or keep correcting the same workflow, turn it into a skill ([Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/guides/best-practices)).

### Evals: start by reading real outputs

The consensus in evaluation practice is to start from error analysis, not generic metrics. Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar name error analysis, which means reading real traces, taking free-form notes, grouping them into failure types, and counting them, as the single most important evaluation activity. Their recommendations: use binary pass/fail judgments instead of Likert scales. Review about 100 traces and label at least 30 of them yourself. Calibrate LLM judges against human labels using the true positive rate (TPR) and true negative rate (TNR). Avoid generic metrics such as BERTScore or ROUGE. They say they spend 60–80% of development time on error analysis and evaluation ([Hamel Husain & Shreya Shankar, Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)). Anthropic's January 2026 article points the same way: start early with 20–50 tasks drawn from real failures, use deterministic graders where possible, and grade the outcome the agent produced rather than the path it took. In other words, check whether the booking actually exists in the database, not whether the agent says it booked. And read the transcripts rather than trusting the scores alone. The article also distinguishes consistency metrics: the probability of at least one success in k tries (pass@k) and the probability of succeeding in all k tries (pass^k). With a 75% per-attempt success rate, the probability of three successes in a row is about 42%. If the latest models score 0%, the task itself is usually broken ([Anthropic, Demystifying evals](https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). For repetitive tasks, hand-tuned prompts can be handed over to metric-driven optimization. Stanford's DSPy, with its self-bootstrapping pipelines, outperformed standard few-shot prompting by more than 25% on GPT-3.5 and by more than 65% on llama2-13b-chat ([DSPy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03714)). In the Prompt Report's case study, an expert reached an F1 of 0.53 after about 20 hours of manual work, while DSPy scored 0.548. The authors concluded that combining automation with human revision was the most successful approach ([Prompt Report](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06608v6)). Scaled down to an individual, it looks like this: write 3–5 binary success criteria, collect 10–20 real inputs, read every output, compare variants on the same set, and keep the passing cases as a regression set to rerun every time you change the model or the prompt. Opinions differ on grading scales. Anthropic's old Console used a 5-point scale, Hamel and Shankar recommend binary judgments, and Anthropic's 2026 article allows binary, weighted, and partial-credit scoring.

## 5. Block injection with structure, and match autonomy to verifiability

### Prompt injection is unsolved, so block it by design

Prompt injection is still unsolved. LLMs cannot reliably distinguish the user's instructions from instructions hidden in the content they read. Willison's "lethal trifecta" describes a session that combines three things: access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and the ability to communicate externally. He points out that no reliable way to prevent injection is known, and that a guardrail product that blocks 95% of attacks would get a failing grade by web security standards. The only safeguard available to users is to avoid combining all three ([Willison, The lethal trifecta](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/16/the-lethal-trifecta/)). Meta's "Agents Rule of Two" reflects the same idea. In a single session, an agent should have at most two of the following: processing untrusted input, access to sensitive systems or private data, and the ability to change state or communicate externally. If all three are needed, it must not run autonomously without human approval ([Meta, Agents Rule of Two](https://ai.meta.com/blog/practical-ai-agent-security/)). OWASP LLM01:2025 distinguishes direct injection from indirect injection hidden in web pages or files. Its mitigations include constraining model behavior, validating output formats with deterministic code, filtering input and output, least privilege, human approval for high-risk actions, segregating and labeling external content, and adversarial testing ([OWASP LLM01:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/)). At the tool level, Claude Code's deny rules (for example, no reading `.env`) persist across all permission modes. They have limits, though. A read deny blocks `cat .env` but not a script that opens the file, and a web-access deny doesn't block `curl` unless the sandbox is enabled. Auto mode is a classifier, so it can be wrong. The docs therefore advise putting "never" rules in deny rules or hooks, and configuring secret-scanning hooks to block on failure ([Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). The reversibility criterion to put in an agent prompt is this: local work that can be undone may proceed. Ask first before actions that are destructive (such as deleting files), hard to reverse (such as a force-push), or visible to others (such as pushes, comments, or messages) ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)). The supply chain is a risk as well. Install skills only from trusted sources and audit less trustworthy ones ([Anthropic, Agent Skills](https://www.anthropic.com/research/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills)); being listed in the Spec Kit community catalog is not an audit or an endorsement ([github/spec-kit](https://github.com/github/spec-kit)). On secrets and personal data, Willison lists exposing secrets and handling personal data among the cautions for vibe coding ([Willison, 2025-03-19](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Mar/19/vibe-coding/)). South Korea's Ministry of the Interior and Safety (MOIS) ChatGPT guide reportedly also covers copyright, personal information, leaks of sensitive information, and answer reliability as points of caution ([korea.kr, snippet-only](https://m.korea.kr/briefing/pressReleaseView.do?newsId=156566491)). Primary sources on personal data and company policy (OWASP LLM02, NIST AI RMF, and others) were not checked in this review.

### Scale autonomy to verifiability and reversibility

In his June 2025 YC talk, Karpathy argued for "partial autonomy" apps with an autonomy slider: build "Iron Man suits" that augment people, not fully autonomous robots. This was confirmed through a secondary summary ([ikyle.me talk summary](https://ikyle.me/blog/2025/andrej-karpathy-software-is-changing-again); [YouTube, Software Is Changing (Again)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LCEmiRjPEtQ)). He said to keep AI on a short leash and work in small increments (reported by Business Insider, reposted by AOL; the link had expired when rechecked on 2026-11-09). In 2026, saying agent output had become noticeably more reliable around December 2025, he shifted his weight toward delegating "macro actions" such as features and refactors ([Sequoia Ascent 2026](https://karpathy.bearblog.dev/sequoia-ascent-2026/)). But the constants (specification, verification, and human understanding) remain. Claude Code's permission ladder runs from manual approval, to auto mode, to a mode that uses only pre-approved tools, to a bypass mode meant only for disposable containers. Manual approval leads to approval fatigue: from about the tenth prompt, people approve without reading. Auto mode stops after 3 consecutive or 20 cumulative blocks ([Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). The decision rule synthesized in the research notes: raise autonomy when success can be checked automatically, actions can be undone (sandbox, worktree, branch), the risk is low, and the lethal trifecta is absent. If any one of these fails, go back to step-by-step review.

There are also times to use AI less, or not at all. In the "jagged frontier" experiment with 758 BCG consultants, on tasks inside AI's capability frontier, GPT-4 users completed **12.2%** more tasks, worked **25.1%** faster, and produced **40%** higher quality. But on a task outside the frontier, 84% got the right answer without AI, while AI users got it right only 60–70% of the time. These figures were confirmed through co-author Mollick's writing and secondary coverage because the HBS and INFORMS source pages were blocked ([One Useful Thing](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/centaurs-and-cyborgs-on-the-jagged); [Organization Science DOI](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838)). In METR's randomized study, 16 experienced open-source developers worked on 246 real issues in repositories they knew well. When allowed to use AI, they were **19% slower.** Before starting, they expected to be 24% faster, and afterward they still believed they had been 20% faster. The METR page says this result is now outdated and links to a February 2026 update, whose figures we did not check ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)). The Stanford AI Index 2026 shows the same jaggedness at scale. Agents' success rate on Terminal-Bench jumped from 20% to 77.3%, yet AI still lags at reading clocks and multi-step planning ([Stanford HAI, AI Index 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/inside-the-ai-index-12-takeaways-from-the-2026-report)). In summary, use AI less when the output can't be verified and the stakes are high; when the task is likely outside the frontier; when an expert is doing small tasks in familiar code; when the data is confidential or restricted by policy; when the goal is to build your own skills; and when doing it yourself is faster than writing a prompt and reviewing the output.

### Outsourcing your thinking undermines learning

The clearest causal evidence on learning comes from a Wharton-led PNAS study. In a field experiment with about 1,000 high school students in Türkiye, GPT Base, a ChatGPT-like tool, raised practice performance by 48% but lowered performance on a later exam taken without AI by **17%**. GPT Tutor, designed to give hints instead of answers, raised practice performance by 127% without hurting exam performance ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/); [PNAS bibliographic record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:122:y:2025:p:e2422633122)). The 48% and 127% figures come from coverage of the 2024 working paper; the 17% decline is what the PNAS abstract confirms. Harvard's CS50 turned this lesson into policy. Its own AI, the CS50 Duck, is designed to guide students toward solutions rather than hand them over, and commercial AI that writes answers for students may not be used on assignments ([CS50x academic honesty policy](https://cs50.harvard.edu/x/honesty/); [CS50 SIGCSE 2024 paper](https://cs.harvard.edu/malan/publications/V1fp0567-liu.pdf)). The MIT Media Lab preprint "Your Brain on ChatGPT" reported that among 54 participants, the group that wrote essays with an LLM had the weakest brain connectivity, the lowest sense of ownership over their essays, and difficulty quoting their own writing. The group that wrote on their own first and used the LLM later showed higher recall and activation ([arXiv 2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)). However, a formal comment criticizing its sample size and EEG methodology has been published, so it should be treated as a contested early signal ([Comment, arXiv 2601.00856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00856)). The practical principle: think or draft first, then use AI. If you are learning, ask for hints, explanations, and quizzes rather than answers. Karpathy's line sums up the principle: "You can outsource your thinking, but you can't outsource your understanding." ([Karpathy, Sequoia Ascent 2026 summary](https://karpathy.bearblog.dev/sequoia-ascent-2026/))

## 6. Labs, universities, and Karpathy reached the same conclusion by different routes

Four kinds of sources reach the same conclusion by different methods. Labs keep living documents updated with the instructions that worked on their own models. Universities test generalizability with controlled and field experiments. Karpathy and other practitioners distill principles from daily use. Where these overlap (clear specification, relevant context, checks that can be run, and final human accountability) is what this repository can teach with the most confidence. The points of divergence are essentially role prompting (labs recommend it; Wharton finds no accuracy effect) and CoT (MIT Sloan recommends it for exposing reasoning; labs say it is unnecessary for reasoning models). In both cases, the conflict dissolves once you distinguish what the technique is being used for.

Academic research also shows a consistent result about how productivity gains are distributed. In the BCG experiment, below-average performers improved by 43% and above-average performers by 17% ([One Useful Thing](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/centaurs-and-cyborgs-on-the-jagged)). In a QJE study of 5,172 customer support agents, AI assistance increased issues resolved per hour by about 15%. Less experienced agents improved in both speed and quality, while the most skilled saw a slight drop in quality ([EurekAlert, QJE](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1071936)). In a preregistered experiment with 776 P&G professionals, individuals using AI (+0.37 SD) matched or exceeded teams without AI (+0.24 SD) ([One Useful Thing, Cybernetic Teammate](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/the-cybernetic-teammate)). In Noy and Zhang's Science study, ChatGPT cut time by 40% and raised quality by 18% on mid-level professional writing tasks. These figures were confirmed through a search summary without opening the paper itself ([Science DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586)). In short, AI raises the floor for novices and is an optional tool for experts, who must hold to their quality bar. The "problem formulation over prompting" view cited by MIT Sloan, which argues that the ability to define the problem, its scope, and its boundaries matters more in the long run than phrasing skills, fits the same picture ([MIT Sloan](https://mitsloanedtech.mit.edu/ai/basics/effective-prompts/)). The Prompt Report likewise names collaboration between prompt writers and domain experts as its most important lesson ([Prompt Report](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06608v6)).

Karpathy's practical advice is best collected in his February 2025 video "How I use LLMs." This report, however, did not watch the video directly and relies on third-party summaries that agree with each other ([Tuan-Anh Bui summary](https://tuananhbui89.github.io/blog/2025/karpathy-lec09); [mvark summary](https://mvark.blogspot.com/2025/03/how-i-use-llms-andrej-karpathy.html)). According to these summaries, he recommends the following. Compare models using leaderboards rather than marketing, and check which model is currently active in the UI. Open a new chat when you change topics. Use thinking models for hard math, logic, and coding; search for current information; and Python for exact calculations. Treat deep research results as a draft to verify against primary sources. In his April 2025 MenuGen post, he wrote that the model suggested deprecated APIs, so he pasted the latest documentation directly into the conversation. The genuinely hard parts, he says, were deployment, secrets management, authentication, and payments ([Karpathy, Vibe coding MenuGen](https://karpathy.bearblog.dev/vibe-coding-menugen/)). In his 2025 year-end review, he concluded that reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) produced reasoning ability, but that models have a jagged intelligence: a genius polymath and an easily confused grade-schooler in one body ([Karpathy, 2025 LLM Year in Review](https://karpathy.bearblog.dev/year-in-review-2025/)). In April 2026, he put forward "verifiability" as the key word. Traditional software automates what can be specified, but LLMs automate what can be verified. Outside the domains a model has been heavily trained on, you need to add context, tools, and evals ([Sequoia Ascent 2026](https://karpathy.bearblog.dev/sequoia-ascent-2026/)). The shift in his principles from "the leash" (June 2025) and "the decade of agents" (October 2025, [Dwarkesh Podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lXUZvyajciY)) to "agent orchestrator" (2026) is not a contradiction. It reflects how far the models have come; the constants of specification, verification, and understanding have not changed.

## Conclusion

The core shift in understanding from this review is this: the unit of "how to use AI" is no longer a good sentence but **a pair: a good specification and a good way to check the result**. What fits the evidence is teaching the six elements as a checklist for handing work to a new employee, and teaching the three failure modes with prescriptions matched to each one's mechanism: for misunderstanding, filling the gaps and restarting; for omission, placement and checklists; for fabrication, grounding and external verification. One less widely recognized implication follows. As models improve, the nature of the risk changes. Obvious errors decrease, but plausible errors and human complacency increase. Falling asleep at the wheel in front of high-quality AI, the correlation between greater trust in AI and less critical thinking, and students' confidence that persists even after they are warned all point in the same direction. So verification habits should not loosen as models improve; if anything, they should be institutionalized. Karpathy's principle that "only what can be verified gets automated" and Claude Code's principle of deterministic enforcement translate, for individual users, into one rule: don't hand off work you have no way to check.

There are two practical conclusions for the how-to-use-ai repository. First, lab guidance changes with each model generation (prefill removed, temperature ignored, conciseness defaults changed). So every tip should carry the date it was checked and, where relevant, an "outdated advice" label, and readers should be taught to trim and re-tune their prompts whenever the model changes. Second, the repo's own taxonomy of Misunderstanding · Omission · Fabrication (hallucination) actually gains credibility if the repo states plainly that the taxonomy has no public source and, in exchange, links each failure to the mechanism research in this report.
